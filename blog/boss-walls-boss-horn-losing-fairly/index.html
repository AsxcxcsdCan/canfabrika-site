Boss walls, the Boss Horn and losing fairly

Some bosses are meant to stop you for a while. The trick is making a loss feel like information, not punishment.

By Can · Can Fabrika · 21 October 2026 · 5 min read

Paw & Sword has real boss walls: field bosses that are deliberately much tougher than the regions around them. A wall is supposed to stop you for a while, until your town, your gear and your team grow into it. That is a promise and a risk at the same time. A good wall makes the eventual win feel earned. A bad wall makes players quit.

The difference, I have learned, is mostly in how losing feels. This post explains how the Boss Horn works, why some bosses are walls, and the rules I added so that a lost fight costs time, not morale.

The Boss Horn: you choose the moment

Each region has a field boss. Every monster your heroes defeat in that region fills its boss gauge. When the gauge is full, the Boss Horn is ready, and you decide when to blow it. After a short muster, the boss rises at the region's altar and your heroes come running.

It did not always work like this. An earlier version ran the horn on a plain timer that had nothing to do with what your heroes had done, and some bosses appeared on their own without the player choosing anything. Both felt arbitrary. Tying the horn to a gauge that your hunting fills makes the fight feel like the result of your work, and letting you pick the moment makes it your decision.

Not every hero answers. Heroes who are far below the region's level stay home, so a fresh recruit does not wander into a fight they cannot survive. The fight itself is built to be watched: a banner and screen shake when the boss arrives, telegraphed big attacks with a ring on the ground, a phase change when the boss is badly hurt, and a victory screen that shows exactly what everyone earned.

Why some bosses are walls

Most field bosses follow a smooth difficulty ramp. A few are deliberately much stronger. Those walls do two jobs. They slow the pace at points where the game wants you to deepen, not widen: improve gear, enhance weapons, build up more heroes. And they make the win memorable, because you will remember the boss that held you up for days.

Walls only work if they test the right thing. That is why Hero Training cannot raise heroes past the current region's level cap. If gold alone could break a wall, it would stop being a wall and become a price tag.

What happens when you lose

A field boss fight has a time limit. If time runs out, or your heroes are all knocked out, the boss leaves. You do not walk away empty-handed: heroes are paid for the damage they dealt, and half of the boss gauge comes back, so the next attempt comes sooner than the first.

Then the bot tests found a nasty loop. After a loss, the next region's horn was ready at once. Heroes who were exhausted or miserable answered it, lost again, and some never made it back to town to recover. The town's income dropped to nothing, and the loop could run for days. That was not a wall. That was a trap.

The rules that make losing fair

Three changes broke the loop:

  • Rest after a loss. After a lost fight, the whole town's horn rests for a short while, with a clear message: the heroes are resting after the lost fight. It gives everyone time to eat, sleep and heal before the next attempt.
  • Tired heroes stay home. A hero who is very low on mood, hunger or stamina sees to their needs first and does not walk to the altar. The horn calls the fit heroes, not every hero.
  • A mood floor. Being knocked out in a horn fight costs some mood, but it can never push a hero below a floor. A string of defeats can make heroes grumpy. It cannot drive them into a permanent sulk.

Together these turn a loss into what it should be: information. The boss is too strong for this team right now. The heroes go home, recover and you decide what to improve. There is a natural pause, and then the next attempt is a decision, not an automatic repeat.

That fits a wider rule in the game: heroes never leave your town. You can neglect them and they will sulk and fight worse, but you will never lose a hero to bad luck or a bad week. I explain why in rare heroes on purpose.

Principles for fair losing

Stripped of Paw & Sword specifics, here is what I would tell anyone designing a wall:

  1. Losing should cost time, never permanent progress.
  2. A loss should leave something behind: partial rewards, a partial refund, a shorter road to the next try.
  3. The game should force a short recovery so failure cannot loop.
  4. Only fit characters should be pulled into a fight.
  5. Penalties need a floor, so bad luck cannot snowball.

Takeaway: a boss wall is only as good as the experience of losing to it. Let the player choose when to fight, pay them something for trying, make the town rest after a loss and put a floor under every penalty.
